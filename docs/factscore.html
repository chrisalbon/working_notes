FActScore

Chris Albon, February 14, 2026, Subscribe

FActScore (Factual precision in Atomicity Score) is a metric for evaluating the factual accuracy of long-form text generated by language models. It was introduced by Sewon Min, Kalpesh Krishna, Xinxi Lyu, Mike Lewis, Wen-tau Yih, Pang Wei Koh, Mohit Iyyer, Luke Zettlemoyer, and Hannaneh Hajishirzi in their 2023 paper, "FActScore: Fine-grained Atomic Evaluation of Factual Precision in Long Form Text Generation."

The core idea behind FActScore is simple: break a long generation into individual atomic facts, then check each one independently against a knowledge source. The score is the percentage of atomic facts that are supported.

How It Works

FActScore operates in two stages. First, a long-form generation is decomposed into a set of atomic facts -- short, self-contained claims that each express a single piece of information. For example, given a generated biography that says "Randall Munroe is an American cartoonist and author who created the webcomic xkcd," the decomposition might yield three atomic facts: (1) Randall Munroe is American, (2) Randall Munroe is a cartoonist and author, and (3) Randall Munroe created the webcomic xkcd.

Second, each atomic fact is verified against a knowledge source. The original paper primarily uses Wikipedia as the reference corpus, but the framework is flexible -- any reliable knowledge source can be used. A fact is marked as supported if the knowledge source contains information consistent with it, and not supported otherwise.

The final FActScore is computed as:

FActScore = (number of supported atomic facts) / (total number of atomic facts)

A score of 1.0 means every atomic fact in the generation is supported by the knowledge source; a score of 0.0 means none are.

Why It Matters

Before FActScore, evaluating factual accuracy in long-form text was difficult. Binary judgments ("is this passage factually correct?") are too coarse -- a paragraph might be 90% accurate but get labeled as wrong because of a single hallucinated detail. Human evaluation of long passages is expensive and hard to reproduce. And automated metrics like ROUGE or BERTScore measure surface-level similarity, not factual correctness.

FActScore addresses these problems by providing a fine-grained, interpretable measure of factual precision. Because it operates at the level of individual facts, it can distinguish between a generation that gets almost everything right and one that is pervasively wrong. It also produces a score that is easy to understand: the fraction of claims that are true.

Practical Considerations

The original paper showed that the atomic fact decomposition and verification steps can themselves be performed by language models, making the metric relatively cheap to compute at scale compared to full human evaluation. The authors found that using a strong retrieval model to find relevant Wikipedia passages, combined with an LLM to judge whether each atomic fact is supported, produced results that correlated well with human judgments.

One important nuance: FActScore measures precision, not recall. It tells you what fraction of what the model said is correct, but not what fraction of what it should have said it actually said. A model that generates a single true sentence about a topic would get a perfect FActScore, even though it omitted most relevant information. This is by design -- the metric targets hallucination specifically -- but it means FActScore should be used alongside other measures of completeness and relevance.

FActScore has become one of the standard benchmarks for evaluating factual grounding in language models, particularly for tasks like biography generation, where the facts can be checked against well-documented sources.
